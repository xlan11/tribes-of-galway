Martin · Cross Street and Quay Street

Tigh Neachtáin

The building now called Tigh Neachtáin is quite large and besides that pub and restaurant also includes the Kasbah Wine Bar and Coffeewerk Press. The property is most associated with the Martin family and is at the top of Quay Street in the sencathrú or old quarter. At the bottom of the street lay Martin’s Mill Lane, where the Martins were first recorded in 1365. Three hundred years later in 1657 twelve properties in the area which had been owned by Martins were seized by the English Commonwealth and distributed to soldiers of its army. That so many were so close to the original family business in the Lane suggests descendants slowly spread out from there and that most of the twelve may have being long-time homes of various branches.

Tigh Neachtáin as it is now dates from the late 15th century and may have been among those destroyed during the devastating fire of Friday 2 June 1473. Whatever previously existed was made anew into a very impressive two and a half story structure, the second floor featuring an oriel window on the corner and with some small windows shaped in Iberian styles. What was once an alley on the north side of the building divided it from the building housing Piazza and Pasta plus Nelly clothes.

Tigh Neachtáin was the town house of Richard Martin of Ballinahinch in Conamara either side of 1801 yet associations may go back further in time. His great-grandfather Robert Martin of Ross had his home in the town seized and given to Edward Eyre, an officer in the English Commonwealth army who garrisoned Galway in April 1652. But in June-July 1661 Martin and most other disposed families had hopes that their properties would be returned by letters issued by King Charles II praising their loyalty to him and their bravery during the town’s nine-month siege.

So Martin said to Eyre that he would soon take possession but Eyre replied that because the King’s letters were not sealed by the Lords Justice they were not worth threepence. Martin angrily accused Eyre of demeaning the king’s authority and of treason. This was serious enough that their dispute ended up in the Irish House of Commons where, to Martin’s severe disappointment, Edward Eyre was unanimously acquitted with his loyalty and integrity praised. Worse still, in 1663 Edward Eyre became Mayor of Galway.

The brothers John and Edward Eyre arrived in County Galway with the English Commonwealth army in the 1650s and established what John Cronin described as “one of the most powerful Protestant ascendancy families in the region, if not the country.” Natives of Brickworth in Wiltshire in England, their father Gyles Eyre died in 1655 aged eighty-two having fathered ten son and five daughters. John and Edward were third and sixth sons which mean their inheritances would be minor so they had great incentive to gain Irish lands and did so by any means necessary.

Matters between the families did not much improve in later generations. Edward Eyre’s grandnephew Stratford Eyre was governor of Galway in 1747 by which time Robert Martin’s grandson, Robert, had conformed to the Church of Ireland to save his estates despite being a Jacobite. As he was now a Protestant, Martin was allowed to carry arms and via his servant Darby Brennan sent a pistol and gun into the town for repairs. Governor Eyre had ordered that all Catholic who carried arms in the town or its liberties be

arrested, so Brennan was arrested at the West Bridge on 9 November 1747 and jailed. The case was tried in April 1748 and this time it was a Martin who won with his pistol and gun returned to him.

Still unsatisfied, Martin followed Eyre to London, hit him on the head and told him to draw his sword. Both men were skilled swordsmen but at the end of the ferocious duel it was Governor Eyre who required medical attention. After the 1740s Eyre influence declined and in the following decades a great deal of their property in and around the town was sold. And relations between them and the Martins must have been greatly improved because in 1788 Edward Eyre leased to Richard Martin a house in Abbeygate Street. By the 1770s the property was the town house of the eldest son of Robert the Jacobite, Richard Martin, a larger-than-life character who survived duels and shipwrecks, was present in north America when the War of Independence began and in Paris when the French Revolution took place. He was famous for pursuing what was then considered a highly eccentric issue, the prevention of cruelty to animals.

But he was also a long-time supporter of full civil rights for Catholics. Despite celebrations at his town house on his election in 1826, he was unseated and obliged to flee to France to escape creditors. So he was absent from Ireland when Catholic emancipation became law in 1829.

Tom Kenny wrote that after the Martins, it was “occupied by the O’Connor family and was known as The Bear House. In 1894 … it came into the possession of John Naughton from Park, Spiddal, and his family have been running it ever since. He married Kate Murphy from Carnmore and they had three children, Paddy, Mary and Sarah. John died in 1904 and subsequently, Kate changed the name over the door to the Irish language version. … The Irish language name over the door did not please the Black and Tans who … threatened them and fired shots through the windows on a number of occasions. … One night, the Tans dragged Kate’s son Patrick, a medical student who occasionally helped wounded republicans, out on the street. It is not known what they were going to do with him but his mother followed them out and kicked up such a racket screaming and screeching that a lot of the neighbours came out on the street. This meant too many witnessesso they let him go.

It came under management of Kate’s daughter Sarah and her husband James Maguire whose son Jimmy runs it today.

On 22 July 2022 the two hundredth anniversary of The Ill-Treatment of Cattle Act alias Martin’s Law was celebrated outside Tigh Neachtáin. This was

to recognise it as the first animal welfare legislation in the world and ancestor of all which have since followed.

From “The Tribes Experience of Galway & other notable families” by Adrian Martyn, edited by Pete Kelly.
