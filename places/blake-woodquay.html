Blake · Woodquay

The Blakes at Woodquay

Sometime in the 16th century a castle was built on the east bank of the Gaillimhe upstream from Galway town at Mionlach in Castlegar parish. Almost nothing is known of its early history save that it was owned by Patrick Coleman in 1574 and that it was among castles built on either side of the river during those generations – Tír Oiléan, Newcastle and Dangan. Given that Menlo Castle is little more than a mile upstream from the town, Coleman must have travelled to it from Woodquay in the town suburbs.

The Uí Cholmáin lived in the town since at least the 1490s. The will of Valentine Blake was made 12 July 1499 and he bequested “To the two daugthers of Tomás Ó Colmáin a cask of wine.” Aodh Ó Colmáin alias Odo Coleman was recorded living in a house in the town during 1503-06, possibly as a Wadenship tenant.

A later Thomas Coleman was notary for Galway Corporation in 1558, recorded as living in the town in April 1570 and owned of Menlo Castle in 1574 He was one of a number of men listed as occupying and owning castles in the area that year - Dominick Lynch held Tír Oileáin, Roland Skerrett held Short Castle alias Castlegar, Carrowbrowne was held by DonálÓge Ó hAllmhuráin, plus Newcastle by Marcus Lynch. On Michaelmas Day 29 September 1589 Edmond Colman was allowed by town statute “from thenceforth and for ever afterwards to have the calling of bailiff.”

Notary was a person enabled to act on behalf of others in legal matters and Coleman was used as such by the Corporation, denoting his importance. Where he trained is unknown, but his education was likely paid for by his family which in turned enabled him to purchase land and build a castle.

Sometime during 1591-92 “the castle of Menlo was demolished for hindering of boats to come into town.” Presumably this was while the Colmans owned it. Sometime between then and the 1620s they sold Menlo to Valentine Blake who was very active in the wine trade with Spain and Portugal during the late 16th century, so much so that he was described in 1592 as the richest man in Galway. While he inherited land and property from his father Walter Blake and mother Julia Browne son of James, he used his profits from the wine trade to buy lands throughout the county and Menlo was one such purchase. Elected a Member of the Irish Parliament in 1613, to mark his rise into the upper ranks of society in 1622 Valentine Blake bought a baronetcy from King James VI and I. The king only did so to gentlemen who had a minimum income of one thousand pounds a year. King James allowed at least two hundred men across the three kingdoms to purchase baronetcies during his reign because he was short of cash and did not want to ask three parliaments for financial aid. So naturally, wealthy men like Valentine Blake were allowed to purchase ranks of nobility in exchange for payments to the king.

But a feature of the widening break between Anglo-Irish and New English was their differing Christian denominations. Blake refused to swear the Oath of Supremacy to King James when elected Mayor of Galway in 1611 and went on Catholic pilgrimage to Loretto and Rome during 1616. On his return he paid for the building of the Chap of Saint Mary of Loretto. It was in this chapel he was buried after his death on 2 January 1635. If he died at Menlo then his body would have been conveyed into Galway down river to Woodquay, then across Horse Island to Saint Francis’s Island and the abbey.

His younger brother was the infamous James ‘Spanish’ Blake, a merchant and double agent.

Sir Valentine was succeeded by his son, Sir Thomas, who had served under his father as bailiff of Galway in 1611-12, became MP for Galway town in 163435 and was Mayor during 1637-38. Blake was among those who led the town into rebellion against the English parliament in 1642 only to be killed by an exploding cannon he was inspecting upon the town wall. He was succeeded by his son, Sir Valentine, who was active with the Irish Catholic Confederation and served on the Connacht Council in 1647, in what was up to then first independent Irish state. The Confederation began to collapse with the invasion of the English Commonwealth under Cromwell in August 1649; even so, Galway town held out until April 1652 when after a nine-month siege, Sir Valentine was one of the few Galwegians to sign the Articles of Surrender. He seems to have died later that year and was buried with his father in the Franciscan. That decade all Blake lands and properties were confiscated by the Commonwealth, though after the 1660 Restoration.

Sir Valentine Blake was the 12th baronet, born in 1780 and elected MP for Galway in 1813. He was a supporter of Catholic Emancipation plus fully opening the electoral franchise to Catholics of all classes. He lost his seat in 1820 and after failing to win it in 1830 and 1838, was elected as a MP who supported dismantling the 1801 Union between Ireland and Britain. He remained MP until his death in 1847.

Sunday Boy Blake was the nickname of Sir Valentine Blake, who was fortyone when he succeeded his father Sir John in 1875. By then his finances were at the point where he could not appear in the town in case he would be arrested by bailiffs for his many outstanding debts.

But such writs could not be served on Sundays, so on the seventh day of each week Sir Valentine would leave Menlo Castle by boat from its dock and travel a mile downriver to Woodquay to spend time in the town. But he always made sure to return to Woodquay before midnight to return to the legal safety of Menlo Castle.

Known to his tenants as Sor Váidín a Blác, he once spat on an elderly tenant who could not, or would not, pay rent. The tenant’s son was Tomáisín Laighléis and was so angered at his father’s treatment that he punched Sir Valentine to the ground. The RIC were called and Sir Valentine told the constables he would not purse the matter if Tomáisín came to Menlo and apologised on his knees. “Tomáisín refused, saying that he would rather go to prison until he was carried out in a wooden box.” Lawless was not jailed and Blake was not prosecuted, mainly because Blake was supposed to be too ashamed to go to court and admit his shameful deed.

On 26 July 1910 Menlo Castle caught fire, plainly visible from Woodquay. James Kirwan the coachman escaped by claiming down the ivy. From the ground he saw servants Delia Early and Anne Browne trapped on the roof. Browne plus two locals named Ward and Faherty placed bundles of hay on the ground and implored the women to jump. It was forty feet from the roof to the ground and twenty-five-year-old Delia Early from Parkaveara jumped badly, landing on her head and was killed instantly. Anne Browne landed on her feet and was severely injured but alive and survived.

Unable to escape was Miss Ellen Blake, an invalid. No trace of her body was ever found so when the family returned from Dublin, the ashes of the house was gathered and buried nearby under a cross dedicated to her. The fire marked the end of the Blake’s habitation at Menlo and 1923 the estate was broken up by the Land Commission.

From “The Tribes Experience of Galway & other notable families” by Adrian Martyn, edited by Pete Kelly.
