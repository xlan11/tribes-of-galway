Ó Conchobhair · Ó Flaithbertaigh · Quay Street and Quay Lane

Ó Conchobhair and Ó Flaithbertaigh

Galway begins in1124 when Annála Tigernach reported “Three castles by the Connachtmen, namely, the castle of the river Galway, and the castle of Cúl Maile and the castle of Dunlo.”They were instigated by Toirrdelbhach Ó Conchobhair, King of Connacht and Éire, who was probably born in Tuam and who much later died at his castle in Dunmore.

The area upon which Caistél Bun Gaillmhe stood is now marked by that block of buildings between Quay Lane in the west, Cross Street in the east, Quay Street to the north and Flood Street to the south. Remains of a later castle, almost certainly built on the same site as Dún Gaillmhe, were uncovered in 2017 by archaeologist Frank Coyne on the site of the Aran Sweater Shop at the corner of Quay Street and Quay Lane.

The territory was originally owned by the Ó hAllmhuráinfamily but they had come under the lordship of the Ó Flaithbertaigh lineage, only for they too to come under the kingship of the Ó Conchobhair dynasty, Kings of Connacht.

Yet there were compensations as Toirrdelbhach Ó Conchobhair chose to install them as constables of Casitél Bun Gaillime.

Annála Tigernach reported the next event there: 1125: The two sons of Aineisleisua Eidhin i.e. Flann and the Gilla Ruadh, two kings of Uí Fhiachrach Aidne, were treacherously killed by ConchoboruaFlaithbertaigh at Bun Gaillimhe.

Nothing else was recorded until 1132, a time when Toirrdelbhach Ó Conchobhair hitherto undisputed kingship of Éire was attacked. Under that year Leabhar Mhic Carthaigh stated: A hosting on land by Cormac son of Mac Carthaigh and the nobles of Leath Mogha into ... and Uí Eachach and CorcaLaoighdhe, and the fleet of Leath Mogha [came] by sea to meet them, and they demolished the castle of Bun Gaillmhe, and plundered and burned the town. The defeat of An Cloidhe [was inflicted] on the following day on [the men of] Iarthar Connacht by the same fleet, and Conchobhar Ó Flaithbheartaigh, king of Iarthar Connacht, was killed, with slaughter of his people.

Chronicon Scottorum concurred with a brief note: “The castle of Bun Gaillimhe was burned by the fleet of the men of Mumu and some of west Connacht killed, including uaTaidc of the household.”Annála Tigernach observed that it was part of a coordinated land and sea attack on Connacht by the forces on Munster under the overall control of Conchúr Ó Brian, with his fleet led by Lochlann Ó Lochlainn from the Burren. It added that besides Conchúr Ó Flaithbheartaigh and two Uí Mughróin being killed, “Flaithbheartach Ó Flaithbheartaigh was the admiral of the fleet.”

This crucial detail is the first mention of the Uí Flaithbheartaigh leading ships on the sea, a feature of their lineage from which they would become famous in succeeding centuries. But it also shows that Toirrdelbhach Ó Conchobhair was himself a sea king capable of appointing admirals to lead his fleet. Others to do so in the Kingdom of Connacht were the Uí Mháille of Umhall in what is now west County Mayo, and the Uí Dhubhda or O’Dowds of what Killalla Bay and coastal County Sligo. In factCaistél Bun Gaillimhe may have been the principal base for the Connacht fleet.

Its importance was underlined by the fact that the Uí Bhriain sacked, plundered and demolished Dún Gaillimhe in 1141 and 1149. But it was obviously repaired and rebuilt because the fleet of Dún Gaillimhe was specified as part of the fleet of Connacht which won Toirrdelbhach Ó Conchobhair’s great naval victory off Tory Island in 1154. AnnálaRíochta Éireann states: A fleet was brought by Toirdhealbhach Ó Conchobhair on the sea, round Ireland northwards, i.e. the fleets of Dún Gaillmhe, of Conmhaicne Mara, of the men of Umhall, of Uí Amhalghadha, and Uí Fhiachrach, and the CosnamhaighUa Dubhda in command over them; and they plundered Tir Conaill and Inis Eoghain.

The Cinel Eoghain and Muircheartach, son of Niall, sent persons over sea to hire (and who did hire) the fleets of the Gall Gaeidhil, of Ara, of Ceann Tíre, of Manainn, and the borders of Alba in general, over which Mac Scelling was in command; and when they arrived near Inis Eoghain, they fell in with the other fleet, and a naval battle was fiercely and spiritedly fought between them; and they continued the conflict from the beginning of the day till evening, and a great number of the Connaughtmen, together with CosnamhaighÓ Dubhda, were slain by the foreigners.

The foreign host was defeated and slaughtered; they left their ships behind, and the teeth of Mac Scelling were knocked out.

Toirrdelbhach Ó Conchobhair died at Dunmore during May 1156, aged seventy-eight.

References to Caistél Bun Gaillimhe alias Dún Gaillimhe (‘the Fort of Galway’) stated that in 1161“Demon ships were seen on the Bay of Gaillimh, and they sailing against the wind. The fortress on the day following was consumed by fire.” But it was again rebuilt, no doubt under the supervision of Ó Flaithbheartaigh who seems to have made it one of his headquarters or even home during these generations. It was burned again in 1170 and in 1177 but the next two events concerning the area were not political but natural: 1178: The Galway river dried up, from OilennaCleireachto the sea, from the hour of nocturn till noon on the morrow, and a great causeway of fish was found therein.

1191: The Gaillimh became dry this year, and an axe was found in it[measuring] a hand from one point of it to the other; and a spear was found in it, and the breadth of the blade of this spear was three hands and three fingers; and its length was a hand from the shoulder. Oilenn a Cleireach alias the Isle of Clerics is modern Tóin an Churraigh and Cúl an Oileáin, cut in two during the 19th century for river navigation. They are four miles upstream from the town and where Loch Corrib ends and the Galway river begins. That it was then called the Isle of Clerics suggests a community of such men in the area who were donated the land.

The caistél alias dún from early on must have been accompanied by some sort of settlement. A population would certainly be required to attend to shipping and from whom its defensive forces could be drawn, though many would have been civilians farming and trading.

And if there was a settled population then there must have been a church. While Saint Nicholas’s church is only documented in 1306, this era may be its true foundation.

No more was heard until 1230 when the Kingdom of Connacht was under great strain from internal divisions and ongoing attacks by the English and their Gaelic allies under William de Burgh. In that year there was a great battle at Áth Gaillimhe, ‘the ford of Galway’. De Burgh seized the castle on the east bank but was attacked from the west bank by Ó Flaithbertaigh, who defended area against de Burgh, the Uí Briain and Mhic Carthaigh who ravaged the surrounding countryside.

While he forced them away in 1232 William de Burgh returned and built a castle of his own at Bun Gaillimhe. While Ó Flaithbertaigh attacked and

destroyed it, another was built by de Burgh, where he and his followers surnamed le Blake and de Lynch managed to firmly establish themselves. But it was not even de Burgh but the latter two families and those who joined their community by the Galway River who made the town famous.

From “The Tribes Experience of Galway & other notable families” by Adrian Martyn, edited by Pete Kelly.
