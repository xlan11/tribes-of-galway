Lynch · Market Street

The Lynch Window

For at least two centuries, a story has been told that one Mayor Lynch found his own son guilty of murder and hanged him from the window of his town house. There is even a monument with a window where the event is supposed to have taken place. The story is well-believed and told by town guides today and has the pillars of a dramatic story – except for facts.

James Lynch fitz Stephen was Mayor of Galway for the terms 149394, 1510-11 and 1516-17. He is last mentioned in town records during 1519, so likely died then or in 1520.

Where he was buried is unknown.

Other members of his immediate family were his father Stephen, alive in 1445. James’s son Stephen was bailiff during 1499-1500, Mayor 1509-10, 1523-24 and 1526-27. Later pedigrees gathered by Hardiman state Marcus Bodkin married Sheela, daughter of James Lynch while genealogical material in the Ulster office compiled during 1597-1632 state that a Stephen Lynch – apparently James Lynch’s father – married Joanna Penrise and had a daughter named Margarita Lynch. Almost certainly a brother of James fitz Stephen was Andrew, Mayor in 1498-99 and ancestor of the Lynchs of Lavally. He was documented in 1477 as Andrew Lynch fitz Stephen and died while imprisoned in Dublin in 1523.

These are the known and likely facts concerning Mayor Lynch. The story with which is most known first occurs in a copy of the Collegiate Manuscript, copied from the Latin original in 1674 and which translated reads as follows: Mr. James Lynch fitz Stephen undertook, at his own cost, the building of the choir of the church of the Blessed Virgin in the western part of the town of Galway. It was he, again at his own cost, who caused the collegiate church of Saint Nicholas to be adorned with glass windows, well and richly coloured, in the year of our Lord, 1493.

It was also this James who, without any other form of law, hanged his son, guilty of murder and of broken trust given to the same stranger, from the window of his own house, leaving to posterity a memorable example of sincere fidelity.

Note that it did not identify the stranger by name or nationality, nor where Lynch’s house was located. As the original Collegiate Manuscript no longer exists it cannot now be said if this was part of the text or added to it. The extracts were to confirm Dominic Lynch in the Dominican Order and so carried testaments and testimony concerning the civic history of the family. Yet the story was absent from the testimony Father Daniel Nelly, a native of the town, while another, Archbishop of Tuam James Lynch, stated guardedly that in 1674 the story was “publicly believed throughout all the province.” By 1752 the unidentified stranger had become a Spanish captain murdered on a ship by Lynch’s son. In 1789 Lynch had become Blake, son of a Mayor in the reign of Queen Elizabeth. This version of the story added that the wife of Mayor Blake gathered two hundred of her family to oppose the execution but failed. It was next dramatised in a novel by Reverend Edward Maginn, whose take was used by James Hardiman in his 1820 History of Galway. Hardiman’s highly romantic version popularised the story greater than any before and it is this version which told today. With various improvements!

In the 1960s and 1970s, Galway historian James Mitchell published two articles which examined the Lynch story in exhaustive depth. He noted that what is curious about the story are the sources which does not mention it. It is absent from contemporary town records like Corporation A. While notable events in Galway from the great fire of 1472 to the 1597 siege were reported in Gaelic annála, none mention a Mayor executing a son. The historian Reverend John Lynch was born in the town in 1599 and wrote at least four histories up to his death in 1677, including on his home town, yet never once mentioned the Lynch story. Neither did his student Ruairí Ó Flaithbertaigh, born in 1629 and whose mother Elizabeth Darcy was from the town yet never

mentions it. Neither did Dubhaltach Mac Fhirbhisigh who composed his monumental LeabharnanGenealach in the town during the 1640s and yet also made no mention of such an event.

Surviving extracts from the Franciscan Abby obituary book likewise do not have it. Neither does a manuscript history written by Geoffrey Lynch fitz Dominic in the 17th century and used by Hardiman as a source for his HistoryofGalway. Nor does Corporation Book A.

Most damningly, an older James Hardiman expressed severe doubt that there was any truth to the story. In 1846 he wrote about the 1674 statement: “This testimony, at the time, was highly respectable, as to the popular belief; and though at the present day it may be considered ancient testimony, yet it cannot be taken as conclusive on a transaction which occurred nearly two centuries before it was given.” He alluded to its complete absence from contemporary and later records and concluded that “The omission certainly renders the truth of the story somewhat suspicious.”

A notable absence is from AnnálaRíoghachta Éireann, compiled during 1632-36 by a team led by Mícheál Ó Cléirigh and was intended as a comprehensive record of Irish history up to AD 1616. The team travelled all Ireland compiling material from every source they could find, with Ó Cléirigh doing so in Galway. Not only that, the actual autograph volume for the years 1335-1608 ended up in Galway during the century and was in the possession of Ruairí Ó Flaithbertaigh. Yet under the year 1493 there is no mention of Mayor Lynch hanging his son, or under any year.

Around 1662 Reverend Henry Lynch of Galway made the celebrated Pictorial Map of Galway in Antwerp. It is a detailed study of the town which tells the viewer and reader everything notable about the town. And yet again, what to us is the most famous story of the town is not mentioned. So sometime between the 1640s and 1674 a story grew in the telling about a Mayor Lynch of Galway who was so justly severe that he hanged his own murderous son. How and why it came about, who first told it, remains a mystery. Yet it is a fact that a lie will travel the world twice before the truth leaves the house!

Perhaps its lies in what happened in between, namely the destruction of the old corporation by the English Commonwealth who also stole their houses and properties while evicting them from the town. Just as the term ‘Tribes of Galway’ is supposed to have originated as an abusive term by the Commonwealth soldiers in those years, perhaps the story was a reaction to the same events. Left with almost nothing, they were nevertheless conscious that they came from people “famous to the world for their trading faithfully, discharging their credit, good education, charity and hospitality both at home

and abroad.” In fact for many their word was about all they had left – but it is something crucial in business and trading relationships.

In 1661 Geoffrey Lynch fitz Dominic wrote that “in old times they would rather hang themselves than break or discontent strangers”, such was the credit the merchants of Galway held with their word. So perhaps someone came up with the story as an extreme example of the fidelity the merchants when their word was their bond. Perhaps.

In 1848 Father Peter Daly assembled different bits of town architecture dating from the 1500s to the early 1720s and erected it on the supposed site of Mayor Lynch’s house, featuring a window from which his son was supposed to have been hanged in the 15th century yet the window dates from the 16th or even 17th century. And on a site where no house ever stood.

The conclusion of James Mitchell and every other historian who has examined the story is that it is pure fiction and has no basis in fact whatsoever. Yet almost daily during the tourist season tour guides recount the story with great enthusiasm to thousands of fascinated people. Not because they are wilfully lying but because it is a great story which they and their audiences become spellbound. And stories are what people remember and are most invested, not facts or history. That it has been dismissed by historians and shown by historians why it should be dismissed matters not at all. Once people become personally invested in good stories they will happy bend or break facts to keep believing in them, to keep hoping that they are true. In our own time many such alternative facts have been malicious and had hurtful outcomes. The story of Mayor James Lynch fitz Stephen is one classified as mostly harmless.

From “The Tribes Experience of Galway & other notable families” by Adrian Martyn, edited by Pete Kelly.
