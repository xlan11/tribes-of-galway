Browne · Abbeygate Street Upper

Upper Abbeygate Street and Bodkin’s Lane

In July 1663 at the Court of Claims in Dublin appeared “Francis Browne, late of Galway, gentleman” to claim nine properties he had held and six other he claimed through mortgages, all of which had been seized during the 1650s by the English Commonwealth in Ireland.

Browne identified himself as the eldest son and heir of “Andrew Browne fitz Dominick of Galway, deceased”, who had “being seized of all the rest of the premises long before the war, did in consideration of a marriage portion and for other good considerations dated in annis 1616 and 1619 and 1636 convey all and singular the same premises to his own use for life, and afterwards to the use of the claimant and the heirs male of his body, with several remainders to his younger brothers”.

The first property in Galway town which he identified was a house near that of his grandfather Sir Dominick Browne’s mansion; another was “a long house in Bodkin’s Lane in Galway, containing many dwellings, and one other stone house opposite thereunto”. He also declared an interest in Ballybritt Castle with about twenty acres of land there, now famous as the site of the Galway Races.

The mortgages Browne had were enabled by wealth inherited from his father Andrew, which he had inherited from his father Dominick and which Dominick had inherited from his father, William Browne fitz Stephen of Athenry. Because while the Brownes had lived in Athenry since about the 1280s and possessed some property there, Francis’s grandfather and his

brothers were forced by warfare between the Berminghams and the Burkes to migrate to Galway and start all over again.

Andrew must have inherited some of his father’s business acumen because Francis’s case at Dublin identified certain properties which seem to have been inherited from Andrew. But Francis likewise must have been a good merchant too as made plain by the properties he claimed through mortgage, scattered across County Galway in the baronies of Athenry, Dunkellin, Kiltartan and Tiaquin plus one each in counties Mayo and Clare.

What’s important to remember is that until the late 1500s and early 1600s, almost none of the Galway merchants owned any large landed estates nor were they notable landlords. What they owned was overwhelmingly confined to the twenty-five or so acres within the walls of Galway, generally their family houses plus gardens. Their wealth came through business and trade, not war or theft like lords, earls and kings. And the confines of Galway meant their fortunes accrued rather than being spent. So when the English conquest which began in 1534 finally ended in 1603, they were land poor but cash rich. Landlords out in the county, on the other hand, were land rich but cash poor. So in the first half of the 17th century there was truly enormous numbers of land transactions made, purchases and mortgages, leases and loans. Unfortunately there were other agendas at play, namely the land greed of New English colonists plus the avaricious corruption of the Stuart kings of the three kingdoms, James VI and I and his son Charles I. James disliked having to go to the English parliament for funds, so got around this by refusing to call parliament and indulge in other ways of raising money. He sold knighthoods, baronetcies, lordships, earldoms, and every mark of nobility he could think of or invent to those willing to pay for it. He awarded income monopolies to favourites who would pay him their due. And he planned plantations across Ireland which would pay dividends to loyal subjects from Scotland, England and Wales which would evict hitherto loyal subjects in Ireland. The New English in Ireland were generally younger sons of landed gentry with no inheritances or severely impoverished middle-class men with nothing to lose and everything to gain by brutal means. They were exemplified by the Earl of Cork, Richard Boyle, who arrived in Ireland from his home in Canterbury in June 1588 and by his death in 1643 had through marriage, warfare, land speculation and fraud had been ennobled and gained hundreds of thousands of acres across Munster. Oliver Cromwell later remarked that if there had been a man like Boyle in every Irish province, there would never have been an Irish rebellion in 1641. But this was to grossly misunderstand that it was the avarice of men like Boyle which caused that rebellion. The theft, corruption and what

Irish Catholics saw as blasphemy on the part of the New English were what drove them to rebellion and the creation of the first independent Irish state. Francis Browne was much lower down the social scale from any lord, baronet or even a knight. Even so his court claim showed that three generations worth of hard work, inheritances and business acumen plus a strong dash of gombínism could result in a very enviable property portfolio. And in a reflection of that, plus his lack of involvement in the troublesome politics of the era, the court found that the crown had no claims on his properties. This did not mean nobody else did, and in an Ireland where New English authority was contested but actual, such claims were rarely considered settled. Suffice to say there is no record of Francis Browne regaining all his properties and with the defeat of the Jacobites in 1691, the English ascendancy in Ireland was fully secured.

From “The Tribes Experience of Galway & other notable families” by Adrian Martyn, edited by Pete Kelly.
