French · Market Street

Sir Peter French’s mansion

Peter French fitz Valentine was known as Peter Óge to distinguish him from his grandfather Peter, who was Mayor in 1565. His great-great-great grandfather Walter French was the first of the family to settle in the town in the early 1400s. French was one of the bailiffs during 1589-90 under Mayor Richard Browne.

He was elected Mayor in August 1616 and would have served in office from 29 September that year to 29 September 1617 but refused the office because of

the Oath of Supremacy. As a result Pierce Lynch took his place and French was fined one hundred pounds.

During those years there was serious difficulty in governing the town because relatively few were willing to take the Oath of Supremacy and serve as Mayor. Besides Peter French, Oliver Browne and Thomas Browne both refused in 1609, as did Valentine Blake in 1611 and Peter Lynch in 1615. In March 1619 French made a little-known yet highly interesting intervention concerning Irish economic sovereignty. According to Raymond Gillespie, … for the greater part of the period between 1603 and 1641 Ireland appeared to have had a surplus in the balance of trade … yet … remained a poor country … French’s conclusion [was] that the central problem … was a severe deficit in the balance of capital flows and hence a shortage of coin … his solution … recommended the establishment of a mint in Ireland to hold foreign coin and bullion … since no mint existed in Ireland there was no incentive for merchants trading abroad to bring back foreign currency or bullion which could be reprocessed into Irish coin at home. They tended to bring back goods … The central problem was that the Kingdom of Ireland, while politically distinct from both Scotland and England, was through the recent conquest ruled from London. The Kingdom had a Parliament in Dublin, but its legislation was always superseded by that of the imperial parliament at Westminster in London. Furthermore, all its legislation and policies were uppermost for the benefit of the New English colonists.

Peter French’s proposal was “an effective devaluation of the Irish currency which would boost exports and curb imports, the net effect of which would be an increase of coin and bullion brought into the country, which could then be processed through the newly established mint.” In his own words, French saw this as brining in “old plate, ingots of gold and silver, Spanish reals, pistoletts in great plenty.” He further explained: “The thing which hinders all sorts of craftsmen … is only the want of money”; economic sovereignty and security would set the public peace of the kingdom … become tradesmen or other good members in the commonwealth and … enable … their children to learning and other employments at home and so many will not wander about the world at the devotion of foreign princes as now they do out of all parts of that kingdom.

By saying this French was alluding to social capital, that the country’s primary wealth was its nation and by investing in them could provide a state worthy enough to afford and nurture them.

The mint was never established and partly in consequence the nation suffered greatly when economic crises’ hit. Matters were only worsened by the terrible famines of the mid-to-late 1630s, on top of which came the political disintegration of the three kingdoms from 1638. In the case of Ireland, the matters French outlined were underlying causes to the 1641 Rebellion and the atrocities which resulted.

Interestingly, two of his sons-in-law were Patrick Darcy and Richard Martin, both who played leading roles in the creation and government of what was the first independent Irish state in 1642, the Irish Catholic Confederation. Some of his thoughts on sovereignty may have influenced them.

French was knighted on 10 August 1623 by Viscount Falkland, the then Lord Deputy of Ireland. His funeral entry in the Ulster’s Office at Dublin reads: “Sir Peter French of Galway, knight, deceased the 27th day of February 1631; he had to wife Mary, daughter of Geoffrey Browne of the aforesaid city, alderman, by whom he had issue Mary, married to Patrick Darcy councillor at law; Margaret alias Maggie, married to Richard Martin esquire; Ellis, married to Sir Robert son and heir to Sir Henry Lynch, baronet; and Jennet French, unmarried. His will appointed executors his wife Dame Mary plus Sir Henry Lynch and Dominick Browne and that he be buried in the Franciscan. His wife Mary made her will on 15 November 1659 and died soon after.

A rather magnificent tomb was made for him, only parts of which now survive as it and many others like it were vandalised by Commonwealth soldiers during the 1650s. In his case it was demolished by John Morgan of Kilcolgan who sold parts of it for chimneys. His mansion house had a doorway which led into a courtyard where there was a four-storey tower house surrounded on four sides by buildings. It passed into the possession of the 4th and 5th Earls of Clanricard. By 1698 it was the Poor Clare Convent, used up to 1712 when they were dispersed and the property was a temporary barracks by 1717. It became an Augustinian nunnery by 1837 and up to 1876. It was unoccupied during 1893-1913 and in 1928 its doorway was removed. After this the entire property was demolished and replaced in 1944 by the Connacht Tribune offices, itself more tastefully modified in the early 2020s. French: Cooke’s Restaurant on Abbeygate Street.

This restaurant features a fireplace dated 1648 celebrating a marriage between a Mr. French and a Miss Bodkin.

Born in 1489, Seán an tSalainn French was Mayor of Galway 1538-39 and a great-grandson of Walter, the first French in Galway. His father, Edmond fitz Robert, was third son of four while his grandfather Robert fitz Walter was youngest of four sons. So his branch were not the senior line nor could they be expected to be the wealthiest.

Yet Seán an tSalainn became the most well-known among the early generations of the lineage because of the great mercantile wealth he made. His nickname denoted ‘John of the Salt’ due to the quantities he successfully imported to Galway over the course of many voyages, probably from the salt flats along coast of western France.

Besides using his wealth for his own benefit, Seán used it to pay for religious works such as a new wing to Saint Nicholas’s church within the walls and a chapel at the abbey of Saint Francis outside the walls. He also made what was called John French’s Chamber on a set of arches over the river just north of the West Bridge. Though its purpose is uncertain.

Seán French’s family included four sons who became Mayors of Galway: Dominick in 1568, Peter in 1576, Robuck in 1582 and Marcus in 1604. He was Mayor again in 1609 Marcus French fitz John was Mayor, with bailiffs Martin Font and Christopher Blake. According to An Account of the Town of Galway, … in or about the year [1609] … Marcus French being mayor, that one Andrew French being destitute of all livelihood went with both is sons called Edmond and Geffry for Spain, where on account of their good education, his eldest son Edmond was made receiver of the King of Spain’s plate and his son Geffry was made governor of a considerable coast in the West Indies belonging to the King of Spain …. Edmond was knighted one of Saint Iago’s knights and general of his fleet coming from the West Indies with his plate, and the said Edmond’s son made the King’s page and afterwards made Captain of Horse in the war against the French King, and there taken prisoner and ransomed by the King with £3000, whose name was Anthony, whose son afterwards died, leaving only one sister, now honourably married in Spain.

Their tale shows that contrary to popular belief, hard times effected the merchants of Galway too and drove them to emigrate. In the cases of this branch of the Frenches their background and education enabled better upward mobility than most of their émigré nation. The traumatic events of the later 17th century would send many more Frenches abroad, not all of whom would do so well.

Joyce: Finnegan’s Restaurant, Market Street.

The Joy family originated somewhere in Wales, possibly the Gwyr peninsula in the south and were first recorded in Ireland in the 1220s. They may have participated in the invasion of the Kingdom of Connacht in the next few years because by the 1270s-80s were recorded in south Connacht William, Philip, Hugh of Seafin and Thomas of Caherskeehaun while in the next generation was recorded Simon Óg of Craughwell and Henry of Caherhenryhoe, places in the Athenry and Loughrea area.

During the 14th century the family appears to have been based primarily in and around Athenry town while a branch under Tomás Rua mac Dabhóic settled in northern Iar Connacht, their territory named after them – DuchasaSeóighe, known in English as Joyce County.

During the latter 1400s several Gaeil and Gaill families from east Galway and south Mayo migrated to Galway and were accepted by the merchant families as fellow freemen and burgesses. Darcys, Kirwans, Nolans, Tierneys and from the Athenry area came the Joyces.

William Joyce settled Galway during the late 1400s. He was likely closely related to Archbishop of Tuam William Joyce fitz Thomas, who was also a native of Athenry. In November 1507 he was a town bailiff when he, bailiff Anthony Lynch and Mayor Arthur Lynch fell off the West Bridge and drowned. He was survived by his wife Agnes Morris and their children James, Henry and Robert.

Henry Joyce became a wealthy merchant and during 1542-43 was Mayor and one of their descendants, Richard Joyce, married Richard Skerrett around 1589, a date which appears on the coat of arms in their house in Market Street. They display an eagle on the background, fronted with a two-bar fess featuring a crescent moon and scallop shells; these were later homogenised into a bland ermine but as symbols tell a story.

The crescent moon, which lies on its back, is often a heraldic symbol of a second son. Scallop shells were awarded to people who had made a successful pilgrimage to Santiago de Compostela in northern Spain. The fess was also found in the Morris arms while the eagle was from the Browne coat of arms. It seems the original Joyce arms was composed by William Joyce and his wife Agnes Morris from elements of their coat of arms. Henry Joyce was a second son, either he or his father a Santiago pilgrim. While her maiden surname is unknown, Henry’s wife Catherine was the widow of Thomas Browne of Athenry and that explains the origin of the eagle.

These facts were re-told as a fairy tale: William Joyce, who was married to Agnes Morris, being on his travels from Italy to Greece, he was taken prisoner by the Saracens, and brought to Africa, from when after a variety of adventures, and undergoing captivity of seven years, he escaped to Spain; while there, his exalted virtues were rewarded by heaven … an eagle flying over his head pointed out to him a place where he discovered vast treasures; with which returning to Galway he contributed large sums towards building the walls, church and other public edifices of the town, he died leaving three sons James, Henry and Robert, and was buried in the Franciscan friary.

Much of this was made up in the 18th century and is based on the life of Richard Joyce, who while emigrating to the Caribbean was captured by Barbary Cosairs and enslaved for seven years in Algiers. But behind this were older stories. One was where a Joyce ancestor, William made pilgrimage to Spain, perhaps made great profits from carrying pilgrims from Ireland to Spain and back. Another was Henry who made a profitable marriage to a Browne (alias the eagle).

It just so happens that four Brownes from Athenry were made freemen of Galway in the years around when Henry was Mayor.

So three different Joyces were blended into one and their story confabulated into a charming tale which overlooked years of hard work, trade voyages and strategic marriages.

How long the house remained owned by the family is unknown as almost all the Tribes had their properties confiscated by the English Commonwealth during the 1650s. Yet Reverend Henry Joyce made the Pictorial Map of Galway around 1662 in Antwerp while John Joyce was a town freeman in 1686, and at least five were recorded living on Abbeygate Street and Shop Street in 1724. One of them was merchant Gregory Joyce, who emigrated to Madrid where he died in 1745; his pedigree states he was a descendant of Henry Joyce.

From “The Tribes Experience of Galway & other notable families” by Adrian Martyn, edited by Pete Kelly.
