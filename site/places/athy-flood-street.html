Athy · Flood Street

Flood Street and Quay Lane

For much of its history between 1124 and the late 1400s, what is now Quay Lane was the town docks. Caistél Bun Gaillimhe was built in 1124 on the corner of Quay Street and Quay Lane while ships would have docked where the houses of Quay Lane are as that was where the river bank was, at the point where the river Gaillimhe met the sea of Loch Lurgan. Hence the location was called Bun Gaillimhe – ‘the mouth of the Galway river’.

But at some point during the 14th and 15th centuries efforts were made to extend the town westwards and create a new docks while building new housing on Botharidirdá Strand. These houses were later demolished and made way for the Fish Market alias Spanish Parade and the road from Wolfe Tone Bridge into Flood Street.

Flood Street was well-named because it was regularly flooded from the river and has done so into living memory. But in the 14th and 15th centuries houses town merchants had properties there, not necessarily homes but given their location on or near the quays, stores for goods outgoing and incoming. The main Athy house seems to have been the one on the site of what is now Saint Augustine House on Saint Augustine Street-Flood Street but the family had a property on the south end of Quay Lane and on its Flood Street side can still be seen the Athy coat of arms, dating from the late 1400s or early 1500s. Among the goods the Athys sold abroad were skins from wolves, stags, hinds, hares, rabbitts and even cats! Clothes, hats, caps and clothes were also manufactured and in that category was the Gaelic brát; this was a great cloak which was a popular export item across Ireland and those in Galway specialised in ones coloured blue. But they came in many forms, types and colours, plus a variety of fashionable fringes.

Barrels of salmon, eels, herrings, honey, corn, butter, cheese, garlic and onions were among the food items exported. Almost none of these goods

could be produced just within Galway town or its liberties so merchants had to act in partnership with communities’ further inland. While the annals frequently mention dynastic violence, documentary evidence from merchants both Gaeil and Gaill show there were deep networks of cooperation working for mutual benefit.

Most poplar of the imports Galwegians imported was wine. As it could not 7779be produced in Ireland or Britain, merchants had to travel to France and Iberia to purchase it. Trade with Brittany, Poitou and Gascony probably dates back to the 13th century but voyages to Spain probably came a little later, likely when the Camino Santiago de Compostela celebrated 1450 as a Holy Year, leading to an upsurge in traffic to and from Ireland. By the 1460s Galway merchants were recorded trading in Lisbon. The connection lasted into the 18th century when emigrant Galwegians established brands like Chateau Kirwan Athy: Saint Augustine’s house, Saint Augustine Street.

Up to 1947 a late medieval building in Saint Augustine Street featured a doorway which bore the Athy arms and the date 1577, signifying that it was once their home. While the house was demolished that year, the doorway was saved and is now held by Galway Museum.

The doorway was made by John Athy and became the home of his son Walter, born around 1560 and died after 1612. He was married to Anne Martin and among their children was Francis, born about 1593 and died about 1644. He too married a Martin, a daughter of George Martin and their marriage stone, dated 163? is kept in storage by Galway Museum.

Their known children were Walter, born 1638; George, born 1642 and called after his maternal grandfather; John, born 1644. Walter’s descendants remained in Ireland and eventually came to own the Renville estate south of Oranmore. But George and John emigrated around 1662 to Maryland in English America, working as indentured servants – on 26 March 1663 Thomas Dent noted to the state authorities he was due land for transporting George Athy plus other indentures.

George worked as a servant for Thomas Dent until 1668 when his indenture ended in November 1668 when the Land Office of Maryland stated “George Attee of Saint Mary’s County [has] proved his right to fifty acres of land due for his time of service performed to Mr. Dent.” In 1670 he married Anne Moorehead, having with her children John and Elizabeth.

Throughout the 1670s Athy was recorded as a farmer trading in crops, hogs, cattle plus at least one court case. His chief crop was tobacco, which one case recorded a year’s crop was worth eight hundred pounds. George Athy’s descendant Darlene Hill shows he used the profits from tobacco to buy more land: Athy’s Hopewell in June 1675, consisting of one hundred acres; Hopewell

in 1680, eighty acres; Stoney Hill in 1688, eighty-eight acres. In 1692 he was living at Nanjamoy in Charles County and sold land on Broad Creek to his church and helped buil an Episcopal Church that year. This shows that despite his family’s religious background, in common with most Irish emigrants in colonial America he changed denomination. Much of the reason for this was because the colonies were overwhelmingly English, so the comparatively Irish conformed entirely religiously and culturally.

Another sign of his standing was that during the 1690s he was made a captain of a company of militia, military units which guarded against native American attacks and were used to kept the African slave population subdued. This responsibility was not one handed lightly, so it indicates Athy was fully accepted by the English authorities as a man who could be trusted to keep the colony white and free, his Irishness notwithstanding.

What records survive for the rest of his life show Athy bought and sold lands – sometimes several hundreds of acres - while being mentioned in some court cases and depositions. He died shortly before 21 December 1709, where his inventory was assessed as twenty-eight pounds, twenty-eight shillings and one pence, witnessed by Sarah Athy and John Athy.

His son John Athy was born about 1676 and by 1691 had his own registered cattle mark, denoting trade in the cattle business. In 1719 he was paying tax and leasing two plantations. In 1711 he married widow Margaret Lewis and they had children Thomas and George before his death early in 1730. Descendants of the family are still to be found in Maryland but have since spread across north America George Athy’s life story was relatively typical for thousands of Irish emigrants who emigrated to 17th and 18th century north America both as free emigrants and bond servants. Their story remains almost unknown today in Ireland because it took place so long ago and well before the great emigrations of the 1800s. Another factor is they so fully assimilated into the English Protestant colonial society at such an early date, and because unlike their 19th century fellow nationals rarely formed distinct Irish communities. But their stories can still be found in colonial records, from which historians can tell their tales.

From “The Tribes Experience of Galway & other notable families” by Adrian Martyn, edited by Pete Kelly.
