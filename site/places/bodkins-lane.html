Bodkin · Bodkin’s Lane, off Abbeygate Street

Bodkin’s Lane

This ran from Lombard Street through what is now Saint Patrick’s School on Mainguard and Lombard Streets. It was called after a family who originated in Athenry who migrated into Galway during the late 1400s. John Bodkin was one of the two town Bailiffs during 1499-1500 and was elected Mayor in August 1518, serving from September 1518 to September 1519with bailiffs Dominick Deane and Martin Lynch fitz James.

He is associated with an infamous town statute made while he was Mayor. Neither Ó nor Mac shall strutt nor swagger through the streets of Galway. So says the popular saying and like most popular beliefs, entirely wrong. Among the statutes passed during his Mayoralty, one stated: “That no man of this town shall host or receive into their houses at Christmas, Easter nor no feast else, any of the Burkes, Mhic Uilliam, Uí Chellaig, nor no sept else, without license of the Mayor and Council, on pain to forfeit five pounds.” It was James Hardiman who in 1820 appended the notorious saying to the statute So contrary to popular belief Irish people were not excluded from Galway. Mayor Bodkin’s statute allowed them in by license.

What the statute really concerned was behaviour. The ClannRiocaird and Mhic Uilliam a Burca plus the Uí Chellaig and several other lineages were powerful and bellicose finte led by warlords who tried to impose their will upon the Galwegians through aggression. There were good reasons for them to build the town walls and maintain them. As recently as 1504 the town was occupied by the ClannRiocaird and only freed by their defeat that August at the battle of Knockdoe by the Earl of Kildare.

Notably, both the Burkes and Fitzgeralds were Gaill or Anglo-Irish, like most of the leading merchant families of the town.

The statute was not anti-Gaelic. Townspeople not survive without civil interactions with local Gaeil lineages such as the Uí hAllmhuáin, Uí

Flaithbertaigh, Uí hEidhinagus Uí Shaughnasaig, so they made efforts to keep good relations with them. And several Gaeil families lived in the town. Colmans, Darcys, Fallons, Kirwans, Nolans, Quins and Tierneys plus dozens of others are recorded in the same classes and castes as the Tribes. All concerned were Gaeilgeoirí, as demonstrated by town records and the fact that Mayor Bodkin’s pedigree was preserved as Gaeilge in LeabharnanGenealach. James Hardiman invented the tradition. Like other fictions it gained great popularity and continues to distort how people think about medieval Galway and its merchant families.

John Bodkin died in 1523, survived by at least six children. One of whom was Christopher, who became Archbishop of Tuam.

Bodkin: Saint Augustine, Abbeygate and Middles Streets plus Eyre Square: While the fictitious execution of a Lynch by his father is commemorated by the so-called ‘Lynch window’ on Lombard Street, actual connections to genuine murders of the Bodkin family lies at the end of that street. On a fireplace in the upper floor of what was until recent years Cooke’s Restaurant in Upper Abbeygate Street are arms of the French and Bodkin families, dated 1648. Living on Abbeygate Street in November 1724 was Widow Bodkin and Nich. Bodkin.

The latter, merchant Nicholas Bodkin of Galway, witnessed a lease and release deed made 22 March 1725 and one of the parties was John Bodkin esquire of Carrabeg. Carrowbeg House is in Belclare near Tuam and Bodkin was a counsellor-at-law, married to Mary Clarke and were parents of Dominick and John. Their townhouse was noted in 1820 by James Hardiman: Bodkin of Carrowbeg mansion in Backstreet, opposite Whitehall and that of another branch of the same name opposite the old Augustinian convent. Backstreet is now Saint Augustine Street so their mansion must have been one of the two large houses at the top of the street facing Whitehall, which is now the back entrance to the Eyre Square Shopping Centre.

The mansion of “another branch … opposite the old Augustinian convent” was in Middle Street. This must be the large building west of Buttermilk Lane or on the east side, now Anthony Ryans.

Councillor Bodkin’s brothers were Oliver and Dominick Dáll. Oliver and his family lived in Carrowbeg while Councillor John mostly lived in Dublin and Galway for his law work, only residing at Carrowbeg for a few weeks a year. In 1737 while on a visit, Councillor John’s son Dominick was found to have died in his sleep.

Councillor Bodkin’s brother Dominick Dáll was so-called because he was blind in one eye. He was also facially scarred by smallpox and had an unsavoury reputation which caused him to be ostracised by his brothers. Concurrently, his nephew John fitz Oliver became estranged from his father and fell in with his heavy-drinking uncle. The two came up with a plan to kill Oliver, his second wife Margery Lycnh and their son Oliver Óge in order for John fitz Oliver to inherit the property from which he had been disinherited. Thus on the evening of 18 September 1741 the two Bodkins and a herd called John Hogan went to Carrowbeg House. They first killed the family dogs lest they bark and wake the household. They murdered the servants sleeping in the outhouse and one sleeping in the hallway. In the guest room they found an unexpected visitor, Marcus Lynch, whom they also murdered. They then murdered Oliver, Margery, and Oliver Óge. Ten to eleven people were murdered that night.

Francis Bermingham (Lord Athenry) was summoned to the house the following day and led the hunt for the murderers, all of whom were soon captured. As custom was to execute murderers near the scene of the crime, they were brought to Belclare crossroads and hung from a tree; Dominick Dáll and John fitz Oliver were gibbetted after death while Hogan’s head was placed on a spike on top of Tuam market house.

Just before they were hanged each made a statement and John fitz Oliver stated that his first cousin John fitz John had murdered his older brother in his sleep by suffocating him at Carrowbeg in 1737. Shocked, John fled the scene on his horse but was apprehended and tried. At the gallows in Galway’s Eyre Sqaure on 20 March 1742 he refused to confirm either his guilt or innocence “and would make no other answer, but forgave Mankind; upon which he was thrown off and in 3 minutes cut down alive, his Privy Parts cut out, and his Bowels taken from him and his Head severed from his body.”

The old Bodkin home in Abbeygate Street still contains the 1648 Bodkin arms while the body of John son of Councillor John Bodkin was possibly among the bodies excavated near the site of the gallows in Eyre Square in the early 2000s. Thomas Bodkin inherited the Carrowbeg estate and was member of Galway’s common council in January 1764. His son and heir was Hyacinth Bodkin. By the early 1820s the estate had passed to the Bodkins of Castletown and Lynches of Shannonbridge.

From “The Tribes Experience of Galway & other notable families” by Adrian Martyn, edited by Pete Kelly.
