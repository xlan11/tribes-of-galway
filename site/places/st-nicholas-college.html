Saint Nicholas’s College

On the 1662 Pictorial Map called “The college of the Priests and Pastors”, this was a three-way building with an enclosed courtyard in front of the main doorway of Saint Nicholas’s church. Called the College House, it was the residence of the Warden of Galway, a bishop in all but name. The office was created by Archbishop of Tuam Donncha Ó Muireadhaigh (himself vicar of Saint Nicholas’s in 1446) to solve jurisdictional issues between the town, the diocese of Annaghdown and archdiocese of Tuam. Archbishop Ó Muireadhaigh raised the church of Saint Nicholas to the status of a college ruled by a Warden and staffed by eight priests or vicars. Most unusually, Ó Muireadhaigh gave their appointments not to himself or his successors but to the Corporation of Galway, who were not clerics but secular men.

Part of the College was built by John Lynch fitz Edmond while Mayor during 1494-95 but it was evidently not finished as John Lynch fitz John saw it necessary in 1496 to finance its completion. It may not have been finished even then as Dominick Dubh Lynch fitz John, who did not die until 1508, was another credited among its founders.

Because they were elected annually, there was as high a turnover among the Wardens as there were with the Mayors. Earliest now known was Sior Énrí Ó Brannagáin alias Sir Henry Brannagain, Warden in 1497. Seán Mac Feorais alias John Bermingham was Warden in 1514 while a document dated 27 March 1522 named Sior Tadhg Ó Casaide alias Sir Thady Cahisy as Warden. Others were Énrí Ó Curraoin in 1525; Seán Ó Diarmada around 1527; Thomas French in 1540; John Bermingham in 1545; 20 August 1549. Diarmaid Ó Nún, 1549; Patrick Blake, April 1551; Sir Tadhg Ó Diarmada, October 1551; Patrick Blake between 1561 and 1563. An order for Blake’s arrest of Blake came in October 1563 because the post had been split into Catholic and Protestant Wardenships. It began on 27 April 1551 when the king, a zealous Protestant, authorised that Patrick Blake become Warden while the vicars choral should be

Patrick Kirwan, Thomas French, Darby Ó Hession, John Talman, Darby O’Rowan, John Dermot, John O’Branagan and Edmund O’Flaherty.

Thus the succession became confused for the rest of the century. Because the English government were now Protestant in denomination, opposing Catholic and Protestant wardens were created. Catholics regained the College and church in June 1643 but their jurisdiction ended with the surrender of the town in April 1652 to the English Commonwealth army under Sir Charles Coote with Colonels Sadlier and Stubbers.

During those years the College House housed a scholar from County Sligo named Dubhaltach Mac Fhirbhisigh. Why precisely remains unknown as he was not in any holy order. It is possible that he was attached to it as a teacher in some capacity. At any rate Mac Fhirbhisigh was in Galway by April 1645 and completed a transcription of SeanchasSílÍr from LeborUa Maine. His transcript of its seventy pages became very useful because the original has since lost four of its fourteen folios. On 8 December 1647 he noted he completed the translation from English into Gaeilge of the Rule of Saint Clare, commissioned by Mother Mary Bonaventure Browne, then Abbess of the Poor Clares of Galway. He gave his place of writing as the College of Galway. Sometime between then and 13 April 1649 Mac Fhirbhisigh began work on his monumental work, LeabharnanGenealach, a massive compilation of Irish genealogies, both Gaeil and Gaill. He completed the section titled RéimRíoghraide Éireann on 8 August 1649, the same week as the inhabitants of Blake’s Castle on the quays became infected with the bubonic plague, an epidemic which over nine months would killed almost four thousand in the town. Mac Fhirbhisigh finished the book’s index on 28 December 1650. Sometime in those years he left the College; in January 1671 he was murdered during a pub brawl in his native county.

An unofficial Catholic wardenship became official in March 1686 with the assent of King James VII and II. Henry Brown was elected Warden, his vicars being Henry Joyce (absent), Michael Lynch, James Fallon, John Rodin, Jerome Martin, Nicholas Nolan and Thomas Lynch – but the church was withheld from them by the Protestant Warden, Doctor Vesey. They seem to have gained it by 1690, when a register suggests Catholic baptisms were once again held within the church – but the army of Queen Mary II and King William III of Orange entered Galway on 26 July 1691 and took possession of the church and college from the then Warden, Reverend John Bodkin.

His successor was Edmund Lynch, who sent the moveable property of the church and college to France for safekeeping. These were converted into money which was regularly sent back to Galway to support its once again unofficial Warden and vicars, carrying out their duties in secret. Only in 1725

was a Catholic chapel openly built on Middle Street under the supervision of Warden Patrick Bermingham. He was succeeded by Hyacinth Bodkin who died in 1749. Bodkin was succeeded by Marcus Kirwan, who was replaced during 1750 by Anthony Blake who in that year rebuilt the chapel on Middle Street into Saint Augustine’s Church. After him were Francis Kirwan, John Joyce to 1783, Valentine Kirwan to 1791, succeeded by John Joyce.

Made around 1792, a list of clergy in the Wardenship named Reverend John Joyce as Warden while priests in Rahoon were Denis and Malachy Mannion. for Saint Nicholas parish were fathers John Joyce and T. Walsh. Saint Francis’s parish, alias Wood Quay, had fathers Bodkin and McHugh while in Saint Augustine Street were James Kirwan and Thomas Tierney. Out in the West which is now the Dominick Street-Henry Street area were fathers Bartholomew Burke and John Fallon. Several other priests were named in the several other parishes of the Wardenship.

Far more documentation exists from the 18th to 19th centuries, showing the positive result gradual relaxing of the Penal Laws was having on the preservation of Catholic parish registers and diocesan records. One document date 16 May 1796 was from Sam Ruxton in Parsonstown, King’s County, complaining to Warden John Joyce about the conducted of Miss O’Shaughnessy of Abbeygate Street because she had jilted him. Another written in Baltimore, Maryland on 5 December 1797 was by Bishop John Carroll of Baltimore, who was at the time the only Catholic bishop in the USA. Bishop Carroll wrote to Warden John Joyces saying that he had received the Warden’s letter by his kinswoman Miss Joyce; that Carroll had married her to a Mr. McDermott; that the couple were now living in Philadelphia. One from around 1800 stated that some Catholics had left local Freemason lodges, that disputes between the clergy and lay patrons were now mostly peaceful, and that the Charity School now had sixty boy pupils.

Warden Joyce was the centre of a case of apparent character assassination early in the 19th century. Catherine O’Brien alleged the Warden had “criminal intercourse” with her in 1785, was illegally elected Warden and other allegations. Among his character witnesses in 1801 was Catherine Blake who had served him for fourteen years as housekeeper. Blake vindicated Joyce’s character and refuted charges brought against by Catherine O’Brien nee Liddane who, claimed Blake, “was a drunken, quarrelsome and importunate slum-dweller.”

Valentine Bodkin was Warden in 1805 and the last Warden, Edmund French, died in 1831. In that year the Pope created a Bishop of Galway, Doctor George Brown.

The Wardenship of Galway had lasted three hundred and forty-seven years. The College House lasted a little longer, completely demolished in 1836. Nothing above ground survives of it, the place where it once stood now taken by Galway’s Saturday Market.

From “The Tribes Experience of Galway & other notable families” by Adrian Martyn, edited by Pete Kelly.
